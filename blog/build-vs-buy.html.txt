<p>A build-versus-buy discussion often begins with the wrong comparison: a subscription price on one side and a developer's estimate for a prototype on the other. Neither describes the full responsibility the team is taking on.</p>
<p>I would frame the decision as a choice about ownership. Which capabilities should the team understand and control deeply? Which responsibilities can a supplier handle well? What remains the team's job even after the purchase?</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Define the capability at the right boundary</h2>
<p>Consider an illustrative notification service. Buying message delivery does not decide when a message should be sent, whether the recipient is authorized to receive it, how preferences work, or how the product handles a failed delivery. Those responsibilities can remain with the product team.</p>
<p>Likewise, building a small sender does not reproduce a mature delivery operation. Compare equivalent scopes. If one option includes monitoring, support, and recovery while the other includes only a successful API call, the decision is not yet well framed.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Compare the lifecycle, not the first milestone</h2>
<p>Estimate integration, testing, documentation, upgrades, incident response, and the time needed to investigate edge cases. Include the opportunity cost of work displaced by the choice. For a purchased service, include configuration, vendor changes, usage variability, and the effort required to understand its failure modes.</p>
<p>Use ranges where uncertainty is high rather than a single precise-looking total. The purpose is to find assumptions that could change the decision, not to make a speculative spreadsheet appear exact.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Distinguish differentiation from control needs</h2>
<p>A capability can be strategically distinctive, operationally critical, both, or neither. A critical component does not automatically need to be built internally; a distinctive user experience does not require owning every layer beneath it.</p>
<p>Ask which decisions require direct control: data location, response behavior, auditability, integration timing, or the ability to recover independently. Then compare whether each option can satisfy those needs under realistic operating conditions.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Run a bounded integration trial</h2>
<p>Test representative cases, including failures, before committing deeply. For the notification example, examine invalid recipients, duplicate requests, rate limits, delayed delivery, and the route from an external error to a useful product status.</p>
<p>Check access boundaries and data handling with the people accountable for them. A supplier's feature list is not evidence that the configured integration meets the product's requirements. The trial should produce a small operating picture, not just a successful demonstration.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">Price the exit without overbuilding it</h2>
<p>Find out whether data and configuration can be exported in a usable form, what happens to in-progress work, and which assumptions are specific to the supplier. Define the signals that would justify changing the arrangement.</p>
<p>It may be worth keeping a narrow interface around an external dependency. It may not be worth building a universal abstraction for every hypothetical replacement. Flexibility has a maintenance cost; preserve the options that matter to plausible decisions.</p>
<h2 id="section-6">Keep a decision record and a review trigger</h2>
<p>Record the scope, alternatives, important assumptions, chosen owner responsibilities, and reasons for the decision. Add concrete review triggers: a required capability changes, usage moves outside the expected range, or the team can no longer meet its operating commitment.</p>
<p>The same approach applies to model APIs and AI tooling. Buying a model does not outsource evaluation or responsibility for the user workflow. Building one does not guarantee control over the data and dependencies around it.</p>
<p>The useful question is not whether building or buying is more impressive. It is which arrangement lets the team deliver and sustain the capability it has actually promised. Continue with <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/prototype-to-production.html">production responsibility</a> and <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/designing-for-10-year-question.html">preserving options under uncertainty</a>.</p>
