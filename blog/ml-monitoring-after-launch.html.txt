<p>A dashboard can be full of green indicators while a model becomes less useful. Requests still succeed, latency looks normal, and predictions keep arriving. Meanwhile, an upstream field has changed meaning or a new user group is receiving poor results.</p>
<p>I would organize monitoring around four questions: is the service running, are its inputs meaningful, are its outputs good enough, and are users completing the task? Each question needs different evidence and often arrives on a different timescale.</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Service health is necessary, not sufficient</h2>
<p>Monitor request volume, errors, latency, and resource pressure. Include external dependencies and queue depth. A timeout can matter as much as an incorrect prediction when the user must make a decision immediately.</p>
<p>Define success at the right boundary. An HTTP response can be successful while the application returns an empty result or silently falls back to stale data. Measure the user-visible outcome rather than relying only on infrastructure status codes.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Input checks should reflect meaning</h2>
<p>Check required fields, freshness, plausible ranges, and known category changes. In an illustrative shipment model, a weight field switching from kilograms to grams can be numerically valid and operationally disastrous. A type check alone will not catch it.</p>
<p>Use a data contract to specify units and availability. Quarantine or explicitly degrade when a critical check fails. Silently filling a broken field with a default may keep the service online while making every prediction less interpretable.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Drift is a question, not a verdict</h2>
<p>A changed input distribution can indicate seasonality, a new customer population, instrumentation changes, or a data defect. It is evidence that something changed, not direct proof that predictive performance worsened.</p>
<p>Choose reference windows that fit the task and investigate alerts in context. Do not trigger automatic retraining simply because a statistic moved. If the new data is corrupted, retraining can preserve the defect in a new model artifact.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Plan for delayed outcomes</h2>
<p>Some labels arrive days or weeks after a prediction. Track the delay and distinguish immature outcome cohorts from fully observed ones. Comparing this week's partial labels with last month's complete labels can create a misleading performance story.</p>
<p>Where immediate proxies are used, state what they do and do not measure. A user accepting a suggestion is not necessarily evidence that it was correct. Periodic review or later outcome data may be needed to check the proxy.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">Connect alerts to an operating response</h2>
<p>For each alert, write a short runbook: who investigates, what evidence to inspect, what can be paused, and how to restore the previous operating mode. Decide which alerts need immediate attention and which belong in a scheduled quality review.</p>
<p>A practical response to a missing critical feed may be to stop automatic publication and expose a labeled fallback. A response to a gradual segment-level quality decline may be to collect cases, test an intervention, and release it through the normal evaluation process. Those are different operational situations.</p>
<h2 id="section-6">Review the monitoring system itself</h2>
<p>Track false alarms, missed incidents, and whether the documented actions actually worked. Remove alerts that repeatedly produce no decision, or improve their context. An alert that nobody trusts is a weak control even if its underlying statistic is sophisticated.</p>
<p>Monitoring becomes valuable when it changes what the team does. The dashboard is only the interface to that process. Start with <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/data-contracts-ml.html">clear input contracts</a>, connect to <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/product-metrics-decisions.html">user outcomes</a>, and use <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/prompt-changes-production.html">controlled releases</a> for the fixes.</p>
<h2 id="section-7">Separate detection from diagnosis</h2><p>Distinguish covariate shift, a change in input distribution; label shift, a change in outcome prevalence; and concept drift, a change in the relationship between inputs and outcomes. Unlabelled input checks cannot fully identify the last two. Use delayed outcomes, sampled review, and task context before selecting a response.
