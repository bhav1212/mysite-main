<p>A forecasting system has at least two jobs: produce useful estimates and continue behaving intelligibly when its data or environment changes. Model selection addresses part of the first job. The second depends on fairly ordinary engineering.</p>
<p>The title is not a ranking of forecasting models in 2026. It is an argument for the durable parts of a production workflow: explicit baselines, reproducible evaluation, predictable delivery, and an owner for exceptions. A new architecture still has to earn its place within that workflow.</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Begin with a small, inspectable comparison</h2>
<p>Use a baseline that reflects the series: a last-observation forecast, a seasonal-naive method, or an established planning rule. Add candidates when their assumptions fit the task and the available data. The relevant question is whether a candidate improves the decision at a reasonable total cost.</p>
<p>Score each candidate at the same forecast origins and horizons. Keep preprocessing and feature availability consistent. A model trained with more recent data is not a clean comparison with a baseline deprived of that information.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Write the temporal contract</h2>
<p>Specify the observation frequency, timezone, aggregation rule, and treatment of missing periods. A missing record is not always a zero. A delayed update is not always a demand collapse. Revision policies also matter when historical figures change after publication.</p>
<p>For an illustrative weekly demand service, the contract might say that each region is reported in the same business timezone and that a forecast is withheld if the latest aggregation has not completed. The exact policy depends on the workflow; the important part is making it explicit.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Evaluate the uncertainty you publish</h2>
<p>Prediction intervals express a range for a future observation under modeling assumptions. They are not a promise that the future will resemble the past. Check empirical coverage on held-out forecast origins, by horizon and relevant segment, and inspect interval width alongside coverage.</p>
<p>If the intervals become too wide to guide the decision, that is useful product information. It may call for a shorter horizon, a different decision process, or manual review rather than a more confident-looking chart.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Design for late data and broken jobs</h2>
<p>Choose an explicit fallback: a last valid forecast labeled with its issue date, a simple baseline, or an unavailable state. Avoid quietly substituting outputs whose meaning differs from the normal prediction. Consumers need to know when the operating mode changes.</p>
<p>Keep the data snapshot, feature definitions, model version, and forecast cutoff together. That makes an unexpected output reproducible. A model artifact without the data and transformation context is often insufficient to explain a historical decision.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">Monitor more than the error</h2>
<p>Track freshness, completeness, forecast delivery, and downstream acceptance. Forecast error may arrive only after the target period ends, while an ingestion failure needs attention immediately. A shift in the input distribution is a diagnostic signal, not proof that the forecast has become inaccurate.</p>
<p>Write an action for each alert. If no one knows whether to pause publishing, switch to a baseline, or inspect a feed, the alert is merely a notification. Retraining should follow evidence and validation rather than fire automatically whenever a distribution changes.</p>
<p>The strongest production stack is not necessarily the smallest or the newest. It is the one whose additional complexity buys a measurable improvement and whose failures have a practiced response. Continue with <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/ml-monitoring-after-launch.html">monitoring after launch</a> and <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/forecasting-air-cargo.html">forecasting around operational decisions</a>.</p>
