Building a Trend Radar with LLMs (Without Hallucinating the Future)

A source-led design for trend discovery: collect evidence, test candidate signals, and use language models to explain rather than invent the story.

LLMsforesight

A language model can produce a convincing list of emerging trends from a short prompt. What the reader cannot easily see is whether the list reflects recent evidence, familiar narratives, or a plausible combination of the two. The writing quality is not a measure of the signal quality.

A more defensible trend radar starts with evidence and makes interpretation a separate step. The pipeline below is a design pattern for that approach, not a claim that any implementation can eliminate hallucination or predict the future.

Give the radar a decision boundary

Choose a domain, a planning horizon, and the decisions the radar should inform. A procurement team evaluating a technology for next year's operations needs different evidence from a research group considering a decade-long program. “Find emerging technology” is too broad to tell either team what belongs on the screen.

Write inclusion and exclusion rules before reading the most exciting documents. Otherwise the system can drift toward whatever is easiest to retrieve or most compelling to summarize.

Preserve provenance during collection

Keep stable source identifiers, publication and collection dates, document type, and extraction quality. Deduplicate before treating volume as a signal. A press release copied across many news sites is not independent confirmation from many organizations.

Track changes in coverage. If a new feed doubles the available corpus, an apparent surge may reflect the pipeline rather than the technology. Compare like-for-like source windows where possible and mark gaps instead of filling them with confident interpretation.

Treat clusters as hypotheses

Clustering can propose groups of related documents. It does not establish that a group is coherent, important, or commercially mature. Inspect representative and borderline documents, and check whether a label remains meaningful when the vocabulary changes.

For an illustrative industrial-inspection cluster, separate research on a detection method from reports of factory deployment. Both are relevant, but they support different claims. Collapsing them into “rapid adoption” would overstate what the corpus shows.

Score observations, not destiny

Possible indicators include changes in publication activity, the diversity of participating organizations, and the appearance of evidence in different source types. Each needs a denominator and a caveat. A small base can produce dramatic percentage growth; source diversity can still conceal a shared upstream story.

Keep the component signals visible rather than treating a composite score as an objective maturity rating. Record the rule used to combine them and inspect whether a ranking changes when reasonable weights are changed.

Ask the model to write within the evidence

Give the model selected documents, computed observations, and a clear distinction between evidence and interpretation. Ask for supported claims, unresolved questions, and counterevidence. Require citations that can be checked at the claim level.

Citation checking is necessary but not sufficient. A retrieved passage can be misread, and a valid link can fail to support the surrounding sentence. Evaluate the synthesis against the sources and use human review where the decision warrants it.

End with a next action

A useful radar item should lead to something proportionate: interview a domain expert, run a limited technical test, monitor a named indicator, or take no action yet. Record what evidence would change that decision. That turns the radar into a learning process rather than a collection of persuasive paragraphs.
