<p>A radar full of interesting technologies can still leave a leadership team with no clearer decision. The problem is not necessarily the research. It is often the missing connection between “this is changing” and “therefore we should do something different.”</p>
<p>I would judge a trend radar by the quality of that connection. The following is a practical meeting design, using an illustrative example rather than claiming evidence from a particular boardroom deployment.</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Begin with a decision already on the table</h2>
<p>Suppose a manufacturer is deciding whether to invest in an automated inspection capability. A generic overview of AI trends is too distant from the decision. The useful questions concern defect coverage, integration with existing equipment, available data, and the cost of missed defects or false rejects.</p>
<p>The radar can help identify which assumptions are changing: sensor capability, supplier maturity, required expertise, or evidence from comparable operating environments. That gives the research a reason to be in the meeting.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Separate the signal from its implication</h2>
<p>A research result, a supplier announcement, and an independently observed deployment are different evidence types. Label them. Then write the implication as a separate statement that the team can challenge.</p>
<p>For example, “a supplier announced support for a new defect class” is an observation about an announcement. “Our production line can now automate this inspection” is an inference that requires local testing. Keeping those sentences apart prevents enthusiasm from quietly becoming a capability assumption.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Give each item a decision-sized brief</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>What changed:</strong> a concise observation linked to its sources.</li>
<li><strong>Why it matters here:</strong> the affected process, option, or assumption.</li>
<li><strong>What remains uncertain:</strong> missing evidence and credible counterarguments.</li>
<li><strong>What to do next:</strong> investigate, test, monitor, or deliberately defer.</li>
<li><strong>Who owns the follow-up:</strong> a named role and a review date.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is not a demand that every signal become a project. “No action, because the relevant constraint has not changed” is a useful decision when the reasoning is recorded.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Use a bounded experiment before a large commitment</h2>
<p>For the inspection example, the next step might be a limited evaluation on representative images, including rare defects and adverse lighting. Define the result that would justify further investment and the result that would end the investigation.</p>
<p>The radar should also record constraints that a technical test cannot settle, such as maintenance responsibility or the availability of a qualified reviewer. Otherwise a promising demonstration can repeatedly return to the meeting without becoming a viable operating capability.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">Measure follow-through, not chart activity</h2>
<p>Counts of added trends and page views describe use of the radar, not its value to decisions. More useful review questions are whether important assumptions were tested, whether actions had owners, and whether the team changed course when evidence changed.</p>
<p>Do not claim that every good outcome was caused by the radar. Decision records can show where it contributed and where other information dominated. They can also reveal a radar that is producing noise, duplicating work, or missing a relevant source.</p>
<p>The radar is an input to strategy, not a substitute for it. Its strongest output is a clearer next decision and a traceable reason for making it. See <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/product-feedback-prioritization.html">evidence-based prioritization</a> and <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/designing-for-10-year-question.html">longer-horizon options</a> for complementary practices.</p>
<h2 id="section-6">Track what the radar actually contributed</h2><p>A decision record can mark which evidence changed an assumption, which recommendation was rejected, and which action was completed. It does not by itself identify the causal effect of the radar on commercial outcomes. For the inspection example, keep the test result, integration constraints, and ownership decision separate. A positive lab result can justify another trial while leaving the production investment unresolved.</p><section aria-labelledby="sources-title" class="post-sources"><h2 id="sources-title">Sources and further reading</h2><ul><li><a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/futures-toolkit-for-policy-makers-and-analysts">UK Government: Futures Toolkit for policy-makers and analysts</a></li>
