<p>“A human will review it” sounds like a strong safeguard until the review queue is full, the reviewer lacks the source material, and the interface offers only a green approval button. A person being present does not automatically make a decision well supported.</p>
<p>A review step is a small product inside the larger product. It needs a user, a task, evidence, authority, enough time, and a way to recover from disagreement. Without those pieces, the control is mostly a diagram.</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Specify what the reviewer is deciding</h2>
<p>In a hypothetical document-processing service, a reviewer might verify extracted invoice fields. That is different from approving payment or deciding whether the invoice is legitimate. Keep those responsibilities separate so an approval at one stage is not misinterpreted downstream.</p>
<p>Define the reviewer's allowed actions: accept a field, correct it, request missing information, reject the item, or escalate it. Record the consequences of each action. The interface should not require reviewers to approve something merely to move it out of their queue.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Put the necessary evidence beside the decision</h2>
<p>Show the relevant source region, the proposed value, and any validation failures together. Make it easy to compare the evidence with the output without repeatedly opening unrelated tools. If the source is unavailable, that should change the review state rather than invite a guess.</p>
<p>Do not overwhelm the reviewer with a full execution log when the task requires a short comparison. Offer deeper detail when needed, and preserve permissions and redaction. Visibility is useful only when it helps the assigned person make the actual decision.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Budget review capacity</h2>
<p>Estimate arrival volume, review time, available staffing, and the consequences of a growing backlog. As a deliberately simple planning example, 120 items at two minutes each require four hours of review before accounting for interruptions, difficult cases, or coordination. That is a workload estimate, not a staffing guarantee.</p>
<p>Decide what happens when demand exceeds capacity. Options might include reducing automated intake, prioritizing time-sensitive cases, or clearly extending the completion window. Quietly bypassing review because the queue is long defeats the purpose of the control.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Test the review experience with errors present</h2>
<p>A demonstration containing only correct suggestions cannot show whether reviewers will catch mistakes. Use representative incorrect, ambiguous, and incomplete cases in a controlled evaluation. Observe whether people notice the problem and whether the available actions let them resolve it.</p>
<p>Measure false approvals, unnecessary rejections, correction quality, and time to resolution. Break down outcomes by error type. A quick average review time can conceal a pattern of accepting outputs without meaningful inspection.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">Do not treat every correction as ground truth</h2>
<p>Reviewers can disagree, misunderstand the task, or introduce an error. Preserve the original output, correction, relevant evidence, and review context. Use quality checks or adjudication where the consequence warrants it before feeding corrections into training or evaluation data.</p>
<p>Keep feedback use separate from the immediate task. A user correcting a record is not necessarily consenting to every possible future use of that correction. Data handling should follow the product's stated permissions and retention policy.</p>
<h2 id="section-6">Close the loop at the user outcome</h2>
<p>The review is complete when the corrected result reaches its intended destination and unresolved cases have an accountable owner. Track that final handoff, not only the number of clicked approvals.</p>
<p>A good human-in-the-loop system makes intervention possible, informed, and sustainable. Pair it with <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/useful-uncertainty.html">clear uncertainty states</a> and <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/black-box-to-glass-box.html">inspectable evidence</a>, rather than treating the reviewer as a universal fallback for everything the automation cannot handle.</p>
<h2 id="section-7">Keep service time separate from staffing</h2><p>Four review hours of work is not the same as four hours of elapsed turnaround. Arrivals can be bursty, reviewers handle other work, and difficult cases take longer than the mean. Measure the service-time distribution and backlog age, and keep a route for unresolved or contested records. A second reviewer or adjudication process may be needed for important labels; agreement between two people is useful evidence but still not automatic ground truth.</p><section aria-labelledby="sources-title" class="post-sources"><h2 id="sources-title">Sources and further reading</h2><ul><li><a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300233">Microsoft: Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction</a></li>
