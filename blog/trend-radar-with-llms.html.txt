<p>A language model can produce a convincing list of emerging trends from a short prompt. What the reader cannot easily see is whether the list reflects recent evidence, familiar narratives, or a plausible combination of the two. The writing quality is not a measure of the signal quality.</p>
<p>A more defensible trend radar starts with evidence and makes interpretation a separate step. The pipeline below is a design pattern for that approach, not a claim that any implementation can eliminate hallucination or predict the future.</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Give the radar a decision boundary</h2>
<p>Choose a domain, a planning horizon, and the decisions the radar should inform. A procurement team evaluating a technology for next year's operations needs different evidence from a research group considering a decade-long program. “Find emerging technology” is too broad to tell either team what belongs on the screen.</p>
<p>Write inclusion and exclusion rules before reading the most exciting documents. Otherwise the system can drift toward whatever is easiest to retrieve or most compelling to summarize.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Preserve provenance during collection</h2>
<p>Keep stable source identifiers, publication and collection dates, document type, and extraction quality. Deduplicate before treating volume as a signal. A press release copied across many news sites is not independent confirmation from many organizations.</p>
<p>Track changes in coverage. If a new feed doubles the available corpus, an apparent surge may reflect the pipeline rather than the technology. Compare like-for-like source windows where possible and mark gaps instead of filling them with confident interpretation.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Treat clusters as hypotheses</h2>
<p>Clustering can propose groups of related documents. It does not establish that a group is coherent, important, or commercially mature. Inspect representative and borderline documents, and check whether a label remains meaningful when the vocabulary changes.</p>
<p>For an illustrative industrial-inspection cluster, separate research on a detection method from reports of factory deployment. Both are relevant, but they support different claims. Collapsing them into “rapid adoption” would overstate what the corpus shows.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Score observations, not destiny</h2>
<p>Possible indicators include changes in publication activity, the diversity of participating organizations, and the appearance of evidence in different source types. Each needs a denominator and a caveat. A small base can produce dramatic percentage growth; source diversity can still conceal a shared upstream story.</p>
<p>Keep the component signals visible rather than treating a composite score as an objective maturity rating. Record the rule used to combine them and inspect whether a ranking changes when reasonable weights are changed.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">Ask the model to write within the evidence</h2>
<p>Give the model selected documents, computed observations, and a clear distinction between evidence and interpretation. Ask for supported claims, unresolved questions, and counterevidence. Require citations that can be checked at the claim level.</p>
<p>Citation checking is necessary but not sufficient. A retrieved passage can be misread, and a valid link can fail to support the surrounding sentence. Evaluate the synthesis against the sources and use human review where the decision warrants it.</p>
<h2 id="section-6">End with a next action</h2>
<p>A useful radar item should lead to something proportionate: interview a domain expert, run a limited technical test, monitor a named indicator, or take no action yet. Record what evidence would change that decision. That turns the radar into a learning process rather than a collection of persuasive paragraphs.</p>
<p>Related: <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/bibliometrics-for-beginners.html">interpreting publication signals</a>, <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/patent-signal.html">patent evidence and its limits</a>, and <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/rag-not-silver-bullet.html">why retrieval is not a truth guarantee</a>.</p>
<h2 id="section-7">Preserve the evidence that disagrees</h2><p>For each proposed trend, record supporting and opposing observations, source independence, and the search cutoff. A copied announcement should count as one evidence origin even when it appears on many sites. Use a claim-level source passage and state whether the claim is a measured observation, an interpretation, or a scenario implication. Ask reviewers which missing evidence would change the decision; prompting for counterarguments alone does not make the search complete.</p><section aria-labelledby="sources-title" class="post-sources"><h2 id="sources-title">Sources and further reading</h2><ul><li><a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/futures-toolkit-for-policy-makers-and-analysts">UK Government: Futures Toolkit for policy-makers and analysts</a></li>
