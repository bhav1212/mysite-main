<p>Publication and citation data can help map a research area: who is working on it, which ideas connect, and how the visible activity changes. They cannot, on their own, tell us which technology will succeed commercially or when adoption will happen.</p>
<p>That boundary makes bibliometrics more useful, not less. Instead of treating a rising curve as a prediction, treat it as a structured question that deserves closer reading.</p>
<h2 id="section-1">Define the corpus before counting it</h2>
<p>A search query is part of the measurement instrument. Record its terms, filters, data source, and collection date. Test it against papers you expect to include and papers you expect to exclude. A keyword that becomes fashionable can change the result even when the underlying research activity changes much less.</p>
<p>Include plausible historical terminology and inspect whether broad terms introduce unrelated work. Embeddings may help discover vocabulary, but they also need validation. They are not a substitute for checking what is actually in the sample.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">Publication volume describes visible activity</h2>
<p>Count publications over comparable time windows and explain what kinds of documents are included. A preprint and its later journal article may describe the same contribution. Deduplication choices can materially change the curve.</p>
<p>Investigate changes in source coverage and indexing delays before interpreting a surge or a decline. Comparing the first few months of one year with the full previous year is especially easy to misread. Show absolute counts alongside growth rates so a small denominator remains visible.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">Citations need time and context</h2>
<p>An older paper has had more opportunity to accumulate citations than a recent paper. Citation practices also differ across fields and document types. Raw counts therefore need context before they are used to compare influence.</p>
<p>For a focused investigation, compare similar publication cohorts and inspect the citing work. A citation may be supportive, methodological, critical, or merely contextual. None of those meanings is captured by the count alone. Research attention is not the same measure as technical quality or deployment readiness.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">Networks suggest where to look next</h2>
<p>Co-authorship, institutional participation, and references between topic clusters can reveal useful connections. A newly connected group may deserve expert review. It does not automatically demonstrate commercialization or the formation of a new field.</p>
<p>Organization names and author identities need care. Mergers, name variants, and ambiguous affiliations can create apparent changes that are really entity-resolution errors. Check a sample of the important nodes before turning a network picture into a strategic conclusion.</p>
<h2 id="section-5">A manageable first exercise</h2>
<ol>
<li>Choose a narrow question, such as how a particular inspection method is being evaluated.</li>
<li>Build a documented query and inspect a sample for relevance.</li>
<li>Deduplicate the records and chart comparable publication cohorts.</li>
<li>Read representative papers, outliers, and the work that connects clusters.</li>
<li>Write what the evidence suggests, what it does not establish, and what additional source would help.</li>
</ol>
<p>OpenAlex is one possible scholarly-data source; the linked description explains its scope and entity model. Before automating against any provider, check its current documentation, coverage, and access terms. Do not assume different databases describe an identical research universe.</p>
<p>The useful output is an inspectable map with limitations, not a league table pretending to forecast the future. Combine it with <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/patent-signal.html">other evidence types</a> and preserve those distinctions in <a href="https://blog.bhaveshjain.com/trend-radar-with-llms.html">a source-led trend radar</a>.</p>
